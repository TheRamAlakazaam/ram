Do you ever scroll through your social media feed and see those yearly clickbait articles about how a meteor is

going to “nearly miss” Earth? It’s almost like the recurring debate about the blue and gold dress.

Mixed in with that annual noise, though, are more serious conversations—like the growing discussion around the _right to repair_ and what it really means to truly “own” the products you buy. It feels like every new piece of technology is designed to lock you into an ecosystem. The device you purchase keeps you attached for about five years through software updates, and after that, support drops off. Once that happens, repairs become expensive or impractical, leaving users stuck replacing products instead of fixing them.Some well-known examples of this include Tesla, which restricts how you can repair your own vehicle; the iPhone, which is designed in a way that makes repairs difficult without specialized tools; and Adobe, which has shifted its powerful creative software to a subscription-based model. Even Microsoft contributes to this in its own way, maintaining a heavily bloated operating system with little incentive to streamline it. At one point, even the Start function could spike CPU usage up to 70%—a basic feature that can slow down or crash lower-end machines.If you’ve made it this far and share my frustration, I want to share a few tools and services that my friends and I use to save money, reduce frustration, and improve our workflows. Many of these are open-source alternatives, built and maintained by people who are passionate about creating functional, user-first products.The first recommendation I have, for anyone who doesn’t rely heavily on Adobe software or high-end gaming, is to consider switching to Linux as your operating system—specifically Fedora KDE. It’s one of the more approachable Linux distributions, and it can be customized to look like macOS, Windows, or something entirely your own.

What makes Linux different is control. You’re not locked into a system that decides when to update, what runs in the background, or how your computer should behave. You choose what gets installed, what gets removed, and how your system runs.Performance is another big advantage. Many Linux systems run significantly faster than traditional operating systems, especially on older hardware. A laptop that struggles on Windows can feel completely usable again on Linux because there’s less bloat and fewer unnecessary background processes.There’s also the issue of ownership. With Linux, you’re not tied into a company’s ecosystem. You’re not being pushed toward subscriptions, forced upgrades, or hardware replacements just to keep your system usable. The software is free, open-source, and maintained by communities that actually use the tools they’re building.That said, Linux isn’t perfect. There’s a learning curve, especially at the beginning. Some industry-standard tools—like certain Adobe products—aren’t available natively, and gaming support, while improving, can still be hit or miss depending on what you play. But for a lot of people, those trade-offs are worth it.If you’re curious, the best way to start is simple: try it without committing. You can run Linux from a USB drive or install it alongside your current operating system and switch between the two. Spend a few days using it for everyday tasks—browsing, writing, watching videos—and see how it feels.At the end of the day, Linux isn’t just about saving money—it’s about having a system that works for you, instead of the other way around.
